## Candidate generation and selection

The exploratory set comprised: omission-sensitive sufficiency rewards; image-aware rewriting; explicit region-linked premises; judge shortcut stress tests; visual/nonvisual deletion diagnostics; subgroup-constrained budgets; learned stopping; crossed training attribution; lifecycle break-even modeling; and latent-thought comparison. These AI-generated candidates drew on the supplied context.

Image-aware rewriting, region references, judge stress tests, and deletion diagnostics became components or controls of Direction 1. Learned stopping became a possible implementation within Direction 2 rather than a fourth direction. Lifecycle modeling and crossed attribution became Direction 3. Latent thoughts were retained as related work and deferred because changing representation would obscure the current attribution question. The final directions target different interventions and effects.

Decision criteria were methodological relevance, information gained under a null result, distinction from the checked precedents, and feasible isolation. Selection used qualitative comparison. The user explicitly authorized choosing the strongest direction for the plan.

**Chosen direction: Direction 1, explicit visual-support preservation.** It directly changes the new reward's target, has observable failure/success labels, and can begin with a cheap judge-discrimination study before policy training. The present-step correctness baseline makes its contribution sharper than simply importing a PRM from log 11. Direction 2 is cheaper to diagnose but needs enough independent examples in each group for a tight accuracy floor and overlaps established adaptive reasoning. Direction 3 is essential supporting methodology and can reuse controls, but its factorial cost and workload dependence make it less focused as the primary one-page research proposal.

Adversarial checks were applied at three points: (1) scoping—correct steps can omit support, so use omission negatives and alternative proofs; (2) evidence synthesis—Appendix H defeats a broad “preservation never checked” claim, so restrict the gap to training supervision and policy outputs; (3) draft—small samples cannot prove tight accuracy preservation, so separate useful-effect, precision, and feasibility claims. The strongest remaining objection is that a judge may solve from the image and reward plausible unsupported text. Correct-answer deletion negatives, independent human scoring, and the image/omission-training ablations target that objection.

All design arithmetic was checked: `1,600 + 400 + 1,000 = 3,000`; four reward arms × three seeds = 12 runs; `12 × 25 + 100 = 400` accelerator-hours; three trace conditions × two reward conditions = six cells; `6 × 3 = 18` runs; `18 × 20 + 140 = 500` accelerator-hours. A per-run ceiling includes all simultaneously occupied accelerators, not wall hours multiplied by an unstated device count. The separate 80-hour allocation study needs no policy training. A throughput pilot determines feasible runs within these caps.

For planning sensitivity only, independent paired binary differences at `n = 1,000` have approximate 95% half-width `1.96 sqrt(q/n)`, where `q` is the discordant-pair fraction and the small difference-squared term is omitted. For assumed `q = 0.1, 0.2, 0.4`, the computed half-widths are **1.96, 2.77, 3.92 percentage points**. These assumptions are illustrative; clustering, seed variation, smaller human-audit/subgroup samples, and simultaneous inference require the prospective simulation in the analysis. In particular, the proposed 3-point accuracy gain and 2-point noninferiority margins may remain unresolved at the cap. Use the prospective simulation to assess achievable precision.
